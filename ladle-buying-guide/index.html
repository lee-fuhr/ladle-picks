How to choose the right ladle

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

To buy the best ladle in 2026, focus on material (stainless steel for durability, silicone for nonstick), handle length (a 12-inch handle keeps your hand safe from steam), and bowl size (4 to 6 ounces is versatile for soups and sauces). Avoid unnecessary extras like a strainer unless you often serve broths, and pick a well-reviewed brand like OXO or Rösle for long-term value.

What size ladle do I need?

Most ladles hold between 2 and 8 ounces, but a 4- to 6-ounce bowl hits the sweet spot for everyday use. A 4-ounce ladle is perfect for single portions of soup or sauce, while a 6-ounce version works well for stews and chowders where you want a heartier serving. If you regularly cook for a crowd, a larger 8-ounce ladle saves time, but it can be clumsy for smaller pans. For gravy or thin sauces, a 2-ounce mini ladle is more precise and reduces drips. Consider buying a set that includes two sizes rather than one all-purpose option, since most home cooks rotate between a small and a medium ladle.

What material should I look for?

Stainless steel is the gold standard: it’s durable, heat-resistant, and won’t react with acidic foods. Look for 18/8 or 18/10 stainless steel, which resists rust and stains better than lower grades. A stainless steel ladle with a mirror finish is easy to clean, but a brushed finish hides scratches over time. Silicone-coated or nylon ladles are gentler on nonstick cookware. High-quality silicone withstands boiling temperatures without melting, but it can absorb odors from garlic or turmeric. Wood ladles look charming and won’t scratch, but they require hand-washing and occasional oiling to prevent cracking. My advice: buy a stainless steel ladle for everyday use and a silicone one for your nonstick pots.

Long or short handle?

Handle length is a safety and comfort issue. A short handle (8–10 inches) feels balanced for small saucepans but leaves your hand dangerously close to steam and hot splashes. A longer handle (12–14 inches) keeps your hand well clear of the pot, which is especially helpful when stirring simmering soups or deep pots. Measurement tip: hold the ladle as you would during cooking, your hand should be at least 4 inches from the bowl when the ladle is in the pot. Many cooks prefer a 12-inch handle as a versatile length. Some premium brands like Rösle offer ergonomic handles with a weighted end that reduces wrist fatigue during extended stirring.

Should I look for special features like a straining hole or spout?

A straining hole (or two) in the bowl allows you to serve broth while leaving solids behind. This is genuinely useful for soups with noodles or vegetables if you like to control the broth-to-solid ratio. However, a ladle with multiple small holes is harder to clean and can let fine particles through. A single large hole strikes a better balance. A pouring spout helps you transfer gravy or sauce without dripping down the side of the bowl. It’s a very helpful feature if you regularly fill ramekins or bottle sauces. The trade-off is that a spout can make the ladle less symmetrical, which some cooks find annoying when resting it on a spoon rest. Choose based on your most common task: strainer for soup, spout for sauces.

How does the bowl shape affect performance?

Deep, round bowls (like a small scoop) are best for clear soups and liquids because they hold a consistent amount and don’t spill. Shallow, wide bowls (more like a sauce spoon) work better for thick stews or chili because they distribute the solids more evenly on the spoon. Some designs split the difference with an oval or teardrop shape. A flat-bottomed bowl rests more stably on a plate or spoon rest, while a rounded bottom makes it easier to scrape the last bits from the bottom of a pot. If you often deglaze pans or stir thick mixtures, a slight curve on the bottom helps you reach the corners. I recommend at least one deep ladle for soups and one shallow ladle for stews.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a metal ladle on nonstick pans?

Yes, but only if the ladle is coated with silicone or nylon. Bare stainless steel or aluminum will scratch nonstick surfaces over time. For nonstick cookware, stick to silicone or wooden ladles to preserve the coating.

What’s the best material for cooking hot soup?

Stainless steel is the best overall choice because it doesn’t melt, warp, or leach chemicals. It also stays cleaner longer than other materials. Avoid plastic ladles near boiling liquid; they can deform and release unwanted compounds.

How do I clean a ladle properly?

Most stainless steel and silicone ladles are dishwasher-safe, but check the manufacturer’s recommendation. Wood ladles should be hand-washed with mild soap and dried immediately to prevent cracking. Always scrub the handle joint where food bits can hide.

What size ladle is best for gravy versus soup?

For gravy, a 2-ounce ladle gives you control to drizzle without overpouring and reduces drips. For soup, a 4- to 6-ounce ladle is standard for a satisfying serving. If you only buy one ladle, go with 4 ounces for versatility.

Can I use a metal ladle on cast iron or enameled cookware?

Yes, metal ladles are safe on bare cast iron because the surface is very hard. However, on enameled cast iron, a metal ladle can chip the enamel over time. Use silicone or wood on enameled cookware to avoid damage.

Is a ladle with a straining hole worth the extra money?

Only if you regularly serve broths with solids like noodles or beans. For clear soups or sauces, a solid ladle is simpler to clean and less likely to clog. Many home cooks find a dedicated strainer spoon more useful than a straining ladle.

In shortThe right ladle makes a surprisingly big difference in the kitchen, it’s one of those tools you reach for daily. Focus on stainless steel for durability, a handle length around 12 inches for safety, and a bowl size that matches your most common recipe. Skip gimmicky features unless they solve a real problem you face. A well-chosen ladle will last years and serve you through soups, stews, sauces, and more.
